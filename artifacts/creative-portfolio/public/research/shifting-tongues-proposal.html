Introduction

Bilingualism is one of the most familiar aspects of contemporary life, with nearly half the population of the world making use of more than one language every day (Grosjean, 2010). In India, the multilingual nature of reality has been an established mode of living for centuries. Regional languages, the colonial legacy of English, and Hindi as a national language coexist, resulting in an intricate rich linguistic landscape. For youth, shifting between English, their home language, and other local languages is a common beat. It is evidence not just of continuity with cultural heritage but also with the imperatives of schooling, globalization, and the social lives that they lead. Language is always more than an objective medium. It is always imbued with meaning regarding who we are and where we belong (Norton, 2013). In families, heritage languages can be a context for closeness. Among friends, language use can mark proximity, difference, or even quiet resistance. In schools and workplaces, English frequently signals proficiency, prestige, or conformity with institutional norms. These differences illustrate how the youth make use of language to negotiate identity within extremely distinct environments. Most of the research into bilingualism has concentrated on observable features such as code-switching or language attitudes, with less emphasis placed on how young adults themselves interpret their decisions. This research queries: How do bilingual young adults change language preference between family, peer, and academic environments, and what does this say about their identity negotiation? In order to respond to this, the research adopts an interpretivist stance, utilizing Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA). This is a focus on lived experience and meaning-making, as opposed to general patterns, which lends itself well to capturing the individual importance of using language. The study will investigate not just switching patterns but also the triggers, motivations, and social connotations that animate these choices. It will examine how bilingual young adults connect their language behaviors with notions of belonging, professionalism, intimacy, or resistance and on what basis, as well as how wider forces, including family wishes, peer culture, and institutional norms, influence these decisions. Semi-structured interviews, aided by contextual cues such as language diaries and critical incident reflections, will be used as the data collection tool. The analysis will be an integration of both thematic and narrative approaches, with reflexive memoing and member checking to establish credibility. In the process, the research makes both theoretical and practical contributions. It identifies the ways in which mundane language use relates to identity-making processes, supplementing research in sociolinguistics, psychology, and cultural studies. It also provides applied knowledge for teachers and counsellors who want to design learning spaces that acknowledge and value the multilingual resources students bring with them.

Literature review

Topical Literature Review

Bilingualism and Code-Switching

Contemporary scholarship increasingly frames bilingualism as a dynamic repertoire of communicative resources, accessed contextually rather than as two discrete monolingual systems coexisting in one mind (Grosjean, 2010). While this functional perspective has redirected research from proving linguistic competence to investigating usage patterns, it risks underemphasizing cognitive constraints and structural regularities that still influence bilingual performance. For example, while code-switching is presented as flexible, structuralist approaches (Muysken, 2000; Myers-Scotton, 1993) reveal that grammatical restrictions cannot be entirely overlooked, suggesting that the "freedom" in bilingual practice is mediated by linguistic form. Interactional approaches (Gumperz, 1982; Auer, 1998) advance this by demonstrating pragmatic motivations topic shifts, stance marking, or alignment—but they often rely on short-term conversational snapshots, limiting understanding of longitudinal identity effects. Recent studies extend these findings by positioning code-switching as both indexical and technical, carrying social meanings such as solidarity, prestige, humor, or resistance (Pavlenko & Blackledge, 2004; Canagarajah, 2013). While this sociolinguistic framing captures nuanced identity work, empirical evidence remains uneven: much research privileges English-dominant contexts, leaving heritage or minority language practices underexplored. For instance, while Ramanathan (2005) and Pavlenko (2006) link code-switching into heritage languages with rootedness and into English with upward mobility, these interpretations can oversimplify complex motivations, ignoring hybrid or ambivalent identity constructions. Emerging research on stance-taking and online hybrid registers (Beatty-Martínez et al., 2020; Jehan, 2025; Tenés et al., 2023) highlights creativity, yet methodological reliance on self-report and social media corpora may skew findings toward performative rather than habitual practices.

Family Context

The family represents the earliest site for language transmission and identity formation, yet research critically shows a tension between idealized parental ideologies and actual language practices. Family language policy studies highlight how parental rules and routines shape heritage language retention (Fishman, 1991; King et al., 2008), but critical gaps remain in accounting for children's agency in resisting or negotiating these policies. While parents’ emphasis on schooling, prestige, and mobility influences both language use and cultural identification (Ramanathan, 2005; Curdt-Christiansen, 2016), there is a risk of overstating parental control and underestimating children's adaptive strategies in multilingual settings. In multilingual contexts like India, the tension between heritage languages and English is particularly pronounced (Annamalai, 2004; Ramanathan, 2005). Families employ strategies ranging from enforcing heritage languages at home to incremental transitions toward English predominance. While these studies effectively document parental intentions and intergenerational identity conflicts, they often rely on retrospective reporting, which may obscure the complexities of daily interaction and emotional negotiation. Triangulated studies, which combine parental reports, recorded interactions, and youths’ perspectives, suggest a more nuanced picture: youths actively navigate familial expectations while constructing hybrid identities, highlighting the critical need to center young voices in bilingual research. For our research, youths' own reflections are particularly valuable, as they combine family expectation and individual experience in the construction of bilingual identity.

Peer Context

Peers constitute a key site of identity experimentation and linguistic innovation, yet most studies capture only snapshots of interaction. Ethnographic research demonstrates that adolescents use language to negotiate status, signal belonging, and differentiate themselves (Rampton, 1995; Eckert, 2000). While these findings underscore the performative and social dimensions of bilingualism, there is limited longitudinal insight into how peer norms evolve, leaving questions about the stability and durability of hybrid registers unanswered. For young multilinguals, peers provide spaces for creative and contextually relevant mixing of English and local languages (Canagarajah, 2013; Tenés et al., 2023). While this research highlights agency, it sometimes underplays structural constraints, such as socioeconomic status or institutional language hierarchies, which may circumscribe these practices. In Indian universities, English functions as a lingua franca for lectures, while vernaculars persist in intimate interactions (Annamalai, 2004; McKinley & Galloway, 2022). Social media further facilitates fluid code-switching, yet the majority of studies focus on short-term observation, limiting understanding of how these practices contribute to long-term identity formation. This calls for a more sustained, reflective, and mixed-methods approach to capture evolving norms and peer-driven linguistic creativity.

Academic Context

The prevalence of English-medium instruction (EMI) in higher education presents a paradox: English confers cultural and economic capital but may simultaneously induce stress or marginalize non-native speakers (Coleman, 2006; Phillipson, 1992; Bernhofer, 2022). Research in India underscores how EMI amplifies inequalities based on caste, class, and schooling background (Ramanathan, 2005; Witenstein, 2024). While some scholars advocate translanguaging approaches to valorize students’ full repertoires (García & Wei, 2014; McKinley & Galloway, 2022), implementation remains uneven, and institutional pressures often reinforce monolingual norms. This highlights a tension between aspirational and restrictive linguistic environments, warranting more critical attention to the structural dimensions of language pedagogy.

Identity Negotiation Across Contexts

Across family, peer, and academic domains, identity emerges as relational, fluid, and co-constructed through discourse (Norton, 2000; Pavlenko, 2006). Norton's concept of "investment" and narrative approaches to bilingual identity reveal how language choices reflect projected futures and negotiated selves. However, much of the literature treats identity and language use in contextual isolation, without systematically interrogating how intersecting factors like class, gender, caste, and digital practices influence bilingual expression. Contrastive studies show the same individual enacting distinct roles home-rooted, peer-solidarity, or academically ambitious (Pavlenko & Blackledge, 2004; Annamalai, 2004) yet a critical synthesis of these patterns remains limited, leaving gaps in our understanding of longitudinal identity trajectories and the socio-political dimensions of bilingualism.

Methodological Literature Review

Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)

Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) is a qualitative methodology with its origins in phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography (Smith, Flowers, & Larkin, 2009). Fundamentally, it focuses on how individuals make sense of their lived experiences. Its core components include: (1) Phenomenology, which emphasizes understanding participants’ subjective experiences; (2) Hermeneutics, the interpretative process whereby the researcher makes sense of participants’ meaning-making; and (3) Idiography, a focus on the detailed examination of individual cases before drawing broader insights. IPA typically uses in-depth, semi-structured interviews or other rich qualitative data, and analysis involves iterative, reflective engagement with the data to identify themes that capture both personal meaning and the context in which experiences occur. For young bilingual adults, IPA offers a robust yet critically nuanced framework for examining how language choices are experienced and understood in everyday life. While it allows researchers to move beyond viewing language as a mere skill, a critical perspective highlights that interpretation is never neutral; the meanings individuals assign to language practices are co-constructed with social, cultural, and institutional contexts, and the researcher’s own positionality inevitably shapes analysis (Smith & Osborn, 2015). IPA is particularly useful for investigating context-dependent language shifts such as between family, peer, or academic settings because these shifts are not merely pragmatic but reflect ongoing negotiations of identity, power, and belonging (Pavlenko & Blackledge, 2004). Comparable applications underscore both its potential and its limitations. Oudet et al. (2022) used IPA to explore language choices of bilingual parents of children with autism during COVID-19 lockdowns, revealing highly individualized, contextually embedded decision-making processes, yet their findings also illustrate how temporal and situational factors constrain broader generalization. Similarly, Howard et al. (2019) examined educational experiences of bilingual children on the autism spectrum, highlighting IPA’s strength in capturing subjective meaning, though the approach remains limited by small, non-representative samples. Purpuri (2024) investigated bicultural bilinguals’ experiences of “feeling different” during language switching, demonstrating IPA’s capacity to illuminate affective and identity dimensions, while also raising questions about the extent to which interpretations can be disentangled from researcher assumptions. Overall, these studies collectively affirm IPA’s suitability for exploring bilingual experiences, particularly identity-related and affective dimensions, but a critical stance reminds us that its insights are deeply interpretive, context-bound, and not easily generalizable.

Hermeneutics

Hermeneutics, or the theory of interpretation, is the second crucial philosophical framework for this study. That is a component of IPA. It stresses that human experience, including language use, is never ever simply neutral or functional it is meaning-filled, culturally shaped, historically situated, and subject to one's own point of view. In the bilingual context, hermeneutics provides a framework for critically examining how individuals construct and make sense of their language use, rather than simply documenting observable behaviors. Bilinguals often recount language shifts through stories, metaphors, or commentary that reveal the intertwined nature of identity, emotion, and social context in everyday practice (Pavlenko, 2007). However, a critical perspective highlights that these narratives are co-constructed and mediated: participants’ accounts are shaped by memory, social desirability, and the very act of being interviewed, while researchers’ interpretative frameworks inevitably influence which meanings are foregrounded or marginalized. Thus, hermeneutics exposes both the richness of subjective experience and the limits of assuming direct access to unmediated truth about bilingual practice. Empirical applications, such as Park (2011), demonstrate the utility of hermeneutics in examining how Korean-English bilinguals actively construct identity through language. While such studies foreground participants’ agency, a critical lens draws attention to the tension between participant-led interpretation and researcher-imposed framing, raising questions about reliability, bias, and the representativeness of conclusions. Moreover, while narrative interviews illuminate nuanced identity negotiations, they often focus on individual meaning-making at the expense of broader structural or sociopolitical constraints, such as institutional language hierarchies, socioeconomic status, or cultural norms. Nevertheless, the method’s emphasis on participant-driven sense-making makes hermeneutics particularly valuable for exploring the fluid, relational, and context-dependent identities of multilingual young adults, provided its interpretive and contextual limitations are acknowledged.

Social Constructivism

Social constructivism offers a critical lens for understanding how language use is socially and culturally mediated, highlighting that bilingual young adults’ linguistic choices are not purely individual preferences but are shaped by families, peers, and educational institutions (Vygotsky, 1978). While this perspective successfully foregrounds the relational and context-dependent nature of language, a critical stance raises questions about the extent to which social norms constrain versus enable agency. For instance, evidence shows that adolescents use language strategically to present themselves within social environments (Maxamadyusupovna, 2025); selecting English in classrooms may signal competence and ambition, whereas vernacular use among friends fosters solidarity. However, such interpretations risk overgeneralizing socially meaningful choices, potentially underestimating the influence of structural inequalities, access to linguistic resources, or internalized hierarchies of prestige. Mthombeni (2021) illustrates how bilingual identities are continually negotiated through interaction, reinforcing that language options are socially situated rather than merely habitual. Yet, a critical analysis also notes that socio-constructivist studies often privilege observable interactions over subjective experience, which may obscure internalized motivations, emotional nuances, or identity conflicts that are not overtly enacted. Furthermore, while social constructivism illuminates the co-construction of knowledge, it may underplay macro-level influences such as institutional policies, historical legacies, or power asymmetries that constrain linguistic choices. Despite these limitations, the approach remains valuable for exploring the dynamic interplay between social context, identity, and language use, provided its interpretive boundaries and potential blind spots are acknowledged.

Research Gap and Rationale

Bilingualism has been extensively researched but gaps continue both in terms of topic selection and methodology. One widespread shortcoming is that most of the research focuses on language use in a single context, for instance, family language policy at home, peer language use in schools, or English-medium classroom instruction. Though they are highly informative, these studies do not consider how the same individuals manage changing expectations across various domains of life. Without such a view, one cannot easily understand how young bilingual adults navigate tensions across home, peer, and academic contexts, or how fleeting language changes add up to longer-term identity negotiations. This matter is especially important in postcolonial contexts in urban India and elsewhere, which are less researched than Western immigrant contexts. In India, for example, meanings of English transcend assimilation. It is frequently tied up with caste, class, and educational privilege, and it is not only a medium of communication but also a symbol of mobility and inequality (Annamalai, 2004). Overlooking these dynamics has the potential to create a narrow conception of bilingualism that does not reflect the social hierarchies influencing language practices. Methodological issues also emphasize the necessity for a new approach. A few studies downplay researcher positionality and politics of translation, which can silence critical elements of bilingual experience. If participants are limited to a single interview language or discouraged from code-switching, salient aspects of their lived practice may be lost. Reflexive strategies such as journaling, multilingual interviewing, and participant engagement in translation checks can enhance validity but are not uniformly applied. Moreover, there is minimal integration between traditions: conversation analysis offers micro-level description of interaction, whereas identity studies prioritize wider trajectories and self-concepts. Without integrating these, the interrelation between moment-by-moment code-switching and larger-scale identity work is murky. These gaps are addressed through this study. By tracking the same participants through family, peer, and educational contexts, and by integrating sociolinguistic and psychological approaches, the study will record the multifaceted ways in which bilingual young adults navigate language use in identity negotiations. The design is congruent with its philosophy. Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) will prioritize meanings of language use from the subject's own perspective, while social constructivism will place those meanings in interactional and cultural contexts. The combination of interviews, diaries, and context prompts will enable comparison of expressed preference and observed behavior as well as detailed examination of code-switching as an identity-negotiation performative act. Other options were explored. Discourse analysis might yield rich description of interaction but would entail wide-ranging naturalistic recording, which raises ethical and practical difficulties in closed family or peer settings. Large-scale surveys would capture trends of prevalence but would lose the interpretive and processual aspects so crucial for this research. The selected design is hence an intentional compromise: it sacrifices wide generalizability for deeper interpretive, reflexive, and contextual sensitivity and is eminently capable of answering the question posed and the gaps found.

Methodology & Methods

Philosophical roots

This study is grounded in the interpretivist paradigm, which assumes that social reality is not fixed but constructed through meaning-making and interaction (Creswell & Poth, 2018). Unlike positivist approaches that treat language use as a measurable variable, interpretivism emphasizes subjective accounts and situated practices, making it well suited for exploring bilinguals’ identity negotiations across contexts (Guba & Lincoln, 1994). Ontologically, the stance is relativist: realities are multiple, shifting, and socially produced. For bilingual young adults, this means that the same language indexes intimacy at home, solidarity among peers, and prestige in academic spaces—none of which are inherent but socially constructed (Annamalai, 2004). Epistemologically, the study is subjectivist and intersubjective: knowledge arises through dialogue between participant and researcher, where meaning is co-constructed rather than discovered (Schwandt, 2000). This challenges survey-based approaches, such as the LEAP-Q (Marian et al., 2007), which reduce preference to static frequency counts without engaging identity work. Hermeneutics strengthens this orientation by foregrounding interpretation. Through the hermeneutic circle, the researcher moves between participants’ narratives and their sociocultural contexts, situating personal stories within broader forces such as colonial legacies of English (Gadamer, 1975/2013; Ramanathan, 2005). While hermeneutics risks over-interpretation, reflexive practices and member checking (Guo, 2024) mitigate this, ensuring participants’ voices remain central. Social constructivism further anchors the study by emphasizing that language choices emerge through interactional and cultural contexts (Vygotsky, 1978). Choices made in family, peer, and academic settings reflect not only personal preference but also the pressures of caste, class, and institutional ideologies (McKinley & Galloway, 2022; Witenstein, 2024). This lens resists the universalizing bias of Western bilingualism studies that often ignore postcolonial hierarchies (Anderson, 2022). Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) integrates these roots, combining phenomenology’s attention to lived experience with hermeneutics’ focus on interpretation and idiography’s case-by-case depth (Smith, Flowers, & Larkin, 2009). While IPA is sometimes criticized for focusing too narrowly on individual narratives, embedding it in a constructivist frame ensures that bilingual identity is examined not only as personal meaning but also as socially situated practice. In sum, this philosophical grounding provides coherence: interpretivism captures subjective meaning, hermeneutics situates it in context, and social constructivism ensures that identity is understood as relational. This addresses a clear gap in bilingualism research, which often isolates family, peer, or academic domains rather than examining how young adults navigate across them within the same lifeworld.

Recruiting participants

Participants will be bilingual young adults aged 18 to 25, who are students in universities or colleges in an urban Indian context. These participants will be selected as previous research on bilingualism has been criticized for neglecting the everyday experiences of Indian students who move between languages in everyday life in family, peer, and academic contexts.

Sampling strategy

A purposive sampling approach will be used because the study needs subjects who match the profile of bilingual, higher-education young adults. Purposive sampling enables including individuals who will be able to give rich, appropriate information on the negotiation of bilingual identity in various contexts (Palinkas et al., 2015). To ensure heterogeneity and to obtain a broader range of experience, maximum-variation sampling will be implemented. Variation will be pursued in the areas of (a) first languages (L1s), (b) mother tongue/second language relationships (e.g., Gujarati/English, Hindi/English, or other regional combinations), (c) gender, (d) socio-economic status, and (e) area of study. This variation will enhance the validity and transferability of the results by illustrating commonalities and contrasts between different linguistic and social contexts (Creswell & Poth, 2018).

Sample size and justification

Six to ten participants will be targeted by the study. This number is suitable for qualitative research involving thorough investigation through interviews, diary keeping, and minimal observation. It finds a balance between depth (enabling room to capture cases' complexity) and breadth (enabling sufficient variability to recognize common themes). Saturation—the stage where there are no new themes—is anticipated within this range, but any negative or unusual cases will be kept to expand the analysis.

Inclusion criteria:

Age 18–25; currently enrolled in higher education (college/university) in the study city. Self-identifies as bilingual (uses at least two languages in daily life). Willingness to (a) be interviewed (audio recorded), (b) keep a 2-week language diary

Exclusion criteria:

Severe hearing/speech impairment that prevents participation in audio interviews (unless reasonable accommodations made). Not fluent enough in any of the research languages to consent and reflect (researcher will offer choice of interview language(s)).

Recruitment procedure

Participants will be recruited with a brief screening questionnaire, which will be both online (Google Forms) and paper-based. This questionnaire will validate bilingual status, age, availability, and preferred language(s) for interview. Recruitment materials, including posters and email notices, will be disseminated using campus bulletin boards, student organizations, course WhatsApp groups, and faculty communications. Those respondents who qualify for inclusion will be invited to a short ten-minute screening telephone call, in which eligibility will be verified and initial questions will be asked. Those participants who continue to qualify will be scheduled for complete participation upon assent. Before data are collected, participants will be given an information sheet that explains the aims of the study, the nature of involvement, and confidentiality arrangements. Written consent, or, where more suitable, recorded spoken consent, will be sought. Participants will be given a gesture, like a chocolate, as small token of gratitude, and, where necessary, travel expenses will be reimbursed

Study design

Design

This project will employ a qualitative, multi-method, multiple-case design with a short embedded longitudinal aspect. All participants will be treated as individual cases, studied through three central contexts: family, peer, and school. Data will be collected via semi-structured interviews, language diaries and follow-up or stimulated recall interviews. The 14-day language diary will comprise the longitudinal aspect, giving information on daily changes in the use of language with ecological validity. Through the use of various sources of data, the research will enable self-reported experience and naturally occurring practice to be triangulated, thus increasing the reliability and richness of the findings.

Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)

The analytic approach will be informed by Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography (Smith, Flowers, & Larkin, 2009). Phenomenology will be the basis for attention to participants' everyday experience of language choice, with close attention to how they talk about and make sense of their own practices. Hermeneutics will guide the interpretive process, focusing on circular movement between individual accounts and the wider sociocultural context. This circular process—the hermeneutic circle—will enable the study to understand language change not just as pragmatic choices but also as identity performances. Idiography will ensure that the case of each bilingual young adult is explored thoroughly before looking for wider, common patterns across participants. This multi-layered application of IPA will be especially well matched to the research objectives, as the investigation aims to identify both individual meanings (for example, what being able to speak English may mean at home with a particular participant) and collective themes across cases (like English as a prestige marker in academic contexts)

Philosophical orientation

Social constructivism underpins the design by positing that language preferences are not fixed but co-constructed in interaction with others. The study attends to how meanings of belonging, solidarity, or prestige emerge through participants’ shifting language practices across contexts. Hermeneutics further anchors the design by emphasizing interpretation within historical and cultural lifeworlds, situating bilingual experiences in broader forces such as colonial language hierarchies and institutional expectations.

Units of analysis

The primary unit of analysis is the individual bilingual young adult (case), while the secondary units are the communicative events.

Time framing

The study employs cross-sectional data collection with an embedded 2-week diary window. Each participant is engaged over 3–4 weeks: screening and consent, initial interview, diary maintenance (with a mid-point check) and follow-up or stimulated recall interviews.

Setting

Data will be collected in an urban Indian higher-education context, spanning naturalistic environments such as homes, classrooms, and peer meeting spaces. Where direct observation is not possible, participant-recorded audio or stimulated recall will be used to reconstruct interactional detail.

Data collection

The study will employ multiple qualitative methods to capture the complexity of bilingual young adults’ language practices across family, peer, and academic contexts. The primary method will be semi-structured, in-depth interviews, conducted once or twice with each participant. Initial interviews will last approximately 60–90 minutes, while follow-up or stimulated recall interviews will last 30–45 minutes. These interviews will focus on participants’ language histories, attitudes toward each language, identity narratives, reasoning behind situational language choices, and specific examples of language shifts across different domains. Participants will be free to choose the interview language(s), and code-switching will be permitted to ensure authentic expression. To complement interviews, participants will maintain a two-week language-use diary as an ecological method to document their daily experiences of bilingualism. In these diaries, participants will log episodes where they used different languages or switched codes, specifying the context (family, peer, or academic), interlocutors, purpose (such as politeness, humor, or practicality), and their felt identity or stance during the interaction. Diaries will include structured prompts alongside open sections for free narrative, and participants will be given the option to submit audio diary entries for convenience. To facilitate these methods, a semi-structured interview guide (with prompts for family, peer, and academic episodes) and consent forms with detailed information sheets will be developed. Refer to Appendix A and B. All participant-provided interactions and interviews will be audio-recorded with permission, but video recording will only be done if both parties agree specifically and ethics allow. All transcripts will be verbatim, containing original languages, pauses, laughter, and code-switching, and following a regular convention. Relevant excerpts will be translated into English for analysis purposes, via forward translation followed by independent back-translation for guaranteeing accuracy. Original transcripts will be retained, and a codebook in both languages will be kept to preserve subtle meanings. Examples of code-switching will be retained in the transcript and tagged with their pragmatic functions, i.e., emphasis, quotation, or solidarity. Lastly, strict data handling procedures will be observed. Audio recordings, transcripts, and consent forms will be stored in a secure manner on an encrypted university server with password access, seen by the principal researcher and supervisor alone. Each participant will have a pseudonym assigned, and all direct identifiers will be deleted from data. An individual encrypted file connecting pseudonyms to actual identities will be kept safely so that confidentiality is maintained throughout the study.

Validation strategies

To ensure rigor, the study will adopt multiple strategies across the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Credibility will be established through triangulation, using multiple data sources such as semi-structured interviews, participant diaries, and participant-recorded audio. These will be further supported by follow-up interviews to clarify ambiguities and validate emerging interpretations. Such triangulation will allow the researcher to cross-check accounts across different contexts, thereby reducing the risk of single-source bias and strengthening the trustworthiness of findings. Dependability will be enhanced through the creation of a transparent audit trail that documents every stage of data collection and analysis. A systematically updated codebook will be maintained to track coding decisions and revisions, ensuring that analytic choices remain consistent and well-justified. Additionally, inter-coder checks will be employed where appropriate, inviting peer researchers to review portions of the data and coding framework, which serves both as a safeguard against idiosyncratic interpretations and as a means to improve analytic rigor. Confirmability will be addressed through reflexive practices. The researcher will maintain a reflexive journal to document preconceptions, emotional responses, and decision-making processes throughout the study. This journaling will help monitor how the researcher’s linguistic background and cultural position may influence interpretation. Negative case analysis will also be integrated, deliberately seeking out data that challenges or complicates emerging themes, ensuring that findings remain grounded in participants’ accounts rather than in researcher bias. Transferability will be supported through the provision of thick description, offering detailed contextual accounts of participants’ backgrounds, settings, and communicative practices. This will enable readers to assess the applicability of findings to other bilingual or multilingual contexts. Maximum-variation sampling will also be used to capture a diverse range of experiences within the bounded category of bilingual young adults, enhancing the breadth of perspectives. Thematic saturation will be employed as the stopping rule, ensuring that data collection continues until no new insights or themes are identified across participants. The researcher will remain reflexive throughout the project by explicitly disclosing their own linguistic history, experiences of bilingualism, and positionality. This disclosure will be integrated into the analysis, making visible the interpretive lens through which the data is approached.

Anticipated concerns/ethical considerations

Ethical safeguards include prior approval from the Departmental Research Ethics Committee, informed consent for all interviews, diaries, and recordings, and explicit rights to withdraw or skip questions. Confidentiality will be ensured through pseudonyms, secure encrypted storage, and separation of identifiers from transcripts. Emotional risk will be mitigated with debriefing sheets and counselling referrals, while peer pressure in focus groups will be reduced by emphasizing voluntary participation and confidentiality. Special care will be taken with recording others, ensuring consent or relying on stimulated recall. Translation will be handled transparently, with bilingual coders and retention of original language extracts where necessary. Participants will receive modest compensation without coercion, and consent will specify conditions for the use of anonymized quotes.

Proposed analysis

The study will employ Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) as the primary analytic method. IPA is rooted in phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography (Smith, Flowers, & Larkin, 2009) and is therefore well-suited to the research aim of understanding how bilingual young adults interpret their shifting language preferences across family, peer, and academic settings. Rather than treating language shifts as surface-level behaviours, IPA allows the exploration of the lived meanings participants attach to their choices and the way these meanings contribute to identity negotiation.

Analytic procedure

Data analysis will proceed case by case, following IPA’s established stages. First, transcripts will be read and re-read to ensure immersion in the participant’s account. Second, initial noting will be undertaken, generating descriptive (what is said), linguistic (how it is said), and conceptual (interpretive) comments. Third, emergent themes will be developed within each case, capturing both experiential claims and interpretative insights. Fourth, connections across emergent themes will be sought to produce a case-level thematic structure. This process will then be repeated for each participant, after which cross-case analysis will identify convergent and divergent themes across the sample. Throughout, reflexive memos will document analytic decisions to strengthen transparency and rigour.

Double hermeneutics

A key principle of IPA is the double hermeneutic: participants make sense of their own language practices, and the researcher interprets these interpretations. This is especially relevant in the present study, as bilinguals’ accounts of language choice are themselves acts of identity negotiation. Recognising the researcher’s positionality including their own linguistic background will be essential to avoid uncritical projection of assumptions. Reflexive journaling and peer debriefing will be employed to manage this interpretive complexity.

Linking to theory

Thematic interpretations will be situated within broader theoretical frameworks. For example, moments of convergence and divergence in language choice will be read through Communication Accommodation Theory (Giles, 2008), while narratives of belonging, conflict, or negotiation will be examined through Identity Negotiation Theory (Ting-Toomey, 2005) and Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). This dual-level reading — idiographic detail grounded in participants’ accounts, contextualised by social theories — will enable both depth and conceptual generalisability.

Critical appraisal

While IPA offers depth and interpretive richness, it is not without limitations. Its idiographic focus typically requires a small sample size (6–10 participants), which constrains claims of representativeness. However, the study prioritises depth of insight over breadth, aligning with the research question’s exploratory nature. Another limitation lies in the subjectivity of interpretation: researchers may overimpose theoretical constructs on participants’ meanings. This risk will be mitigated by grounding each interpretive claim in verbatim extracts and by conducting member-checking with participants to ensure credibility. Finally, IPA is labour-intensive, particularly with multilingual data where translation nuances may complicate interpretation. To address this, careful transcription protocols and, where needed, consultation with bilingual peers will be adopted.

Software and practicalities

Data will first be manually annotated to encourage closeness with the text, and then organised using NVivo to manage emergent codes and cross-case connections. A coding trail will be maintained. In conclusion, IPA is warranted as the method of analysis for this study because it enables inquiry into the subjective meaning of language use, is consistent with the social constructivist and hermeneutic approach, and offers a rigorous but relaxed framework for linking individual experience to general theory of identity negotiation. Its restrictions — small sample, interpretive subjectivity, and analytic intensity — are identified and actively countered to facilitate both credibility and scholarly rigour.

Alternative Analysis -Narrative Analysis

Narrative analysis offers a framework for understanding how individuals construct meaning through stories about their lived experiences (Riessman, 2008). In the present study, this approach is particularly relevant, as language preference and code-switching are not merely functional acts but deeply embedded in identity negotiation. By eliciting narratives from bilingual young adults, the analysis can uncover how participants situate their language choices within broader life contexts such as family expectations, peer group belonging, and academic demands. This provides a textured understanding that goes beyond surface-level descriptions of language use. A narrative lens also enables exploration of temporality and continuity in participants’ accounts. Unlike thematic analysis, which fragments data into codes and themes, narrative analysis foregrounds the sequence and structure of the story itself (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000). For example, the way participants recount a “first moment” of realizing their bilingual identity or narrate conflicts with family over language choices reveals how meaning is built across time. Such narrative structuring allows the researcher to see how participants make sense of shifting language preferences not as isolated incidents, but as evolving processes tied to self-concept and belonging. Critically, however, narrative analysis brings certain challenges. One limitation lies in its high interpretive demand; the researcher’s reading of a narrative inevitably imposes frameworks that may not align with the participant’s own meaning (Squire, 2013). Additionally, narratives are inherently selective: participants may omit, exaggerate, or reconstruct experiences, raising questions about authenticity and reliability. In the context of bilingualism, stories may be further shaped by the language in which they are told—possibly privileging one linguistic identity over another. These issues must be acknowledged reflexively to maintain analytical rigor. Another critical aspect is the socio-cultural embeddedness of narratives. Stories about language use are not neutral reflections but shaped by cultural discourses on bilingualism, power, and belonging (De Fina & Georgakopoulou, 2012). For instance, participants may frame English as a symbol of upward mobility in academic contexts, while simultaneously portraying mother tongue use as a marker of intimacy in family interactions. Attending to these narrative tensions can illuminate how identity negotiation occurs at the intersection of personal experience and social structures. Finally, narrative analysis aligns well with the study’s ethical stance by valuing participants’ voices as central knowledge sources. Rather than reducing language practices to frequency counts or functional categories, this approach foregrounds participants’ lived realities, thereby contributing to a more humanistic account of bilingual identity. At the same time, the researcher must remain cautious of over-romanticizing personal stories; critical engagement requires recognizing both the empowering and constraining aspects of narratives in shaping identity.

Critical Evaluation

The proposed study demonstrates considerable coherence between its philosophical foundations, methodological approach, and research aims. By situating bilingualism within interpretivist, hermeneutic, and social constructivist frameworks, the design acknowledges the complexity of meaning-making processes in multilingual contexts. This alignment is a strength, as many studies in the field have treated bilingualism reductively, either as a linguistic competence or a structural phenomenon, overlooking subjective experience and social negotiation (Smith, Flowers, & Larkin, 2009). At the same time, the choice of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) raises questions regarding scope and representativeness. While IPA is well-suited for uncovering nuanced accounts of identity negotiation, its idiographic orientation and small sample size risk limiting broader generalizability. The proposal recognizes this limitation but perhaps underestimates how interpretive subjectivity and linguistic translation might further constrain the validity of findings. The multi-method design, combining interviews, diaries, and stimulated recall, is methodologically rigorous and likely to enhance ecological validity through triangulation. This design goes beyond many earlier studies that have relied solely on retrospective interviews or short-term observations (Braun & Clarke, 2006). However, the integration of these data sources presents practical and analytic challenges. For instance, diarized accounts may differ significantly from interview narratives in terms of reflexivity and depth, raising questions about how the analysis will reconcile potentially divergent data forms. Moreover, the burden on participants to maintain detailed diaries across two weeks could affect compliance and consistency, potentially skewing the richness of longitudinal insights. Another strength lies in the explicit reflexive strategies outlined, including positionality statements, member checking, and bilingual transcription protocols. These measures demonstrate an awareness of the politics of interpretation in bilingual research (Guo, 2024). Nonetheless, while reflexivity is emphasized, the proposal risks over-reliance on researcher-managed strategies at the expense of participant-driven validation. For example, inviting participants into co-analysis or collaborative theme development could further mitigate interpretive bias and enhance authenticity of findings. Without such participatory practices, the double hermeneutic remains vulnerable to over-interpretation by the researcher, particularly given the multilingual complexities of the data. The theoretical contribution of the study is promising, particularly in its attempt to integrate sociolinguistic and psychological perspectives across family, peer, and academic contexts. This ambition addresses a clear gap in the literature, which often isolates these domains rather than analyzing them holistically (Pavlenko & Blackledge, 2004). Still, the theoretical frameworks proposed—such as Communication Accommodation Theory and Social Identity Theory—tend toward the micro- and meso-level. A deeper engagement with macro-level theories, such as linguistic imperialism (Phillipson, 1992) or postcolonial identity frameworks, could provide greater critical depth and situate the findings within broader socio-political hierarchies of language. Without such framing, there is a risk of reproducing descriptive accounts of identity negotiation without interrogating the structural inequalities that underlie them. Finally, while the proposal demonstrates strong alignment between research question and methodological design, some practical and ethical concerns warrant greater scrutiny. Recruiting participants through campus networks may privilege middle-class, English-dominant students, limiting the heterogeneity the study aims to capture. Similarly, while confidentiality measures are well articulated, the use of audio diaries and recordings in intimate family settings could pose unanticipated ethical dilemmas, particularly in cases where consent from secondary interlocutors is not feasible. Addressing these issues in advance would strengthen both the ethical robustness and inclusivity of the project. In sum, the proposal represents a methodologically thoughtful and theoretically grounded attempt to examine bilingual identity negotiation among young adults in India. Its commitment to reflexivity, idiographic depth, and triangulated design are notable strengths. However, limitations concerning generalizability, interpretive subjectivity, participant burden, and structural critique require further attention. By more fully integrating participant-led analysis, engaging macro-level theoretical frameworks, and broadening recruitment strategies, the study could achieve both depth and critical reach, positioning it as a significant contribution to bilingualism research in postcolonial contexts.
